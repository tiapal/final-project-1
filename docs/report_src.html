🌿 Virtual Food Expo

A virtual food trade show where importers and distributors shop for manufacturers from home, with every message translated into the reader's own language.

AuthorBettina Palang (solo project), AI for Managers, Final Project, October 26, 2026
GitHub repohttps://github.com/tiapal/final-project-1
App linkhttps://virtual-food-expo.onrender.com (free hosting: the first visit after a quiet period can take about a minute to wake up, and accounts reset when it restarts)
In one sentenceBuyers describe what they need, an AI Matchmaker finds vendors, and they chat, schedule calls and request samples with each vendor's AI booth assistant, with all messages translated both ways.

2. Motivation

Who has this problem

Food importers and distributors find new suppliers mostly at trade shows. Small and mid-size manufacturers go to the same shows to find buyers. This project started with my dad, a food distributor who used to attend these shows. His anxiety keeps him at home on most days now, so he misses the shows and the suppliers who go with them. He is not alone: anyone who cannot travel, cannot afford to, or is not comfortable in a loud crowd is shut out of how the industry does business.

How people deal with it today, and what it costs

  • Money for exhibitors. At the Summer Fancy Food Show, a 10x10 booth listed at $4,700 in 2025 and $4,900 in 2026 on state pavilion registration pages [1][2]. That is the booth only, before travel, shipping samples and staff.
  • Money for attendees. One itemized example of a four-day national industry show put the cost to the attendee's employer at about $4,000, with a convention hotel at $300 a night and airfare and local transport near $800. The author notes international shows cost much more [3].
  • Too much to see. Anuga 2025 in Cologne reported about 8,000 exhibitors from 110 countries and about 145,000 trade visitors [4]. No buyer can meet everyone in four days.
  • Language. The manufacturer and the buyer often do not share a language. At a show this is handled with gestures, brochures and luck.
  • Access. For people like my dad, the cost is not only money. It is the shows he does not go to at all.

Why anyone should care: better access means more buyers for small makers, better prices for buyers, and fewer people left out because of travel, cost, health or language.

What is different, and why it matters: (1) Language is built into the conversation, so a Bisaya-speaking buyer and a Spanish-speaking maker can talk without a shared language, and every price and quantity is checked so a translation cannot change a number. (2) Each vendor has its own grounded assistant, so buyers get instant answers at any hour, and the assistant cannot reveal the vendor's private bottom price. (3) The next step is one tap: schedule, video call, or request a sample, with approval from the other side and calendar invites. (4) It is designed for people who may be older or less comfortable with technology: large type, high contrast, a store-like layout.

Honest note: I compared against public descriptions and did not hands-on test the competing platforms, so I describe differences in focus and design and make no claim about their every feature.

4. The agents

Four agents, each with its own job and tools

AgentWhat it doesTools it can use
MatchmakerTurns a buyer's plain request into a search, ranks the vendors, and explains each match in one sentence, in the buyer's languagesearch_vendors (filters by product words, category, country, certifications, maximum price, quantity). Anything it names that is not a real vendor is dropped.
Booth assistant (one per vendor)Answers a buyer's questions as that vendor's representative, using only that vendor's public information. Says so when it does not know, and hands discount questions to the vendor's team.quote_price (exact price tier and total, enforces the minimum order), shipping_estimate
TranslatorDetects the language of each message, translates to and from English, translates the interface, vendor descriptions and summaries. Every number, price and unit must survive.A built-in number checker: it compares every number before and after, retries once, and flags the message if they differ. Translations are cached.
Deal briefTurns a chat into a one-page bulleted summary: what the buyer wants, what the vendor offered, what is agreed, what is open, next stepNone (uses only the chat text)

Some important work is plain code, not AI, on purpose: the price calculation, the vendor search filter, the guards on what the booth assistant may say, the approvals for meetings and samples, the calendar files, and login. AI does the language work, and code does the money and the rules.

How the agents are organized

Figure 1. How a chat message travels: (1) the buyer writes, (2) the Translator converts it to English, (3) the Booth assistant reads its vendor's public data and calls its tools, (4) the output guard checks the reply, (5) the Translator converts it back, (6) the buyer reads it in their language. The Matchmaker (search box) and the Deal brief ("Make my summary" button) are separate agents the buyer reaches directly.

Which model, and why

All four agents use gpt-6-luna through the course Portkey key, which is the only model allowed on that key. I call it with reasoning_effort = "none". This was not a free choice: the first search I ran failed with an error saying this model only allows function tools when reasoning is off. It is also the cheapest and fastest setting, and fits tasks like translation and price lookups that do not need long reasoning.

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Reasoning turned off (reasoning_effort = none) on every call, which also made replies fast: English chat replies took 0.6 to 1.1 seconds in my test run.
  2. Translation cache. Each distinct text is translated once per language. I tested this: translating the same sentence a second time made 0 extra model calls (evidence/trace_one_message.json).
  3. Pre-translated interface. The 176 interface labels are translated once for Spanish, Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic and Bisaya and shipped with the app. Before this, the first person to pick a language waited about 25 seconds and paid for the translation.
  4. Small prompts. A booth assistant sees only its own vendor's public data (about 700 tokens), never the whole catalog. Chat history is limited to the last 12 messages.
  5. Prices and search are code, not AI. The tier price, total and vendor filtering run as ordinary code and cost nothing.
  6. Hard caps (see Safety): 80,000 tokens per session, 1.5 million per day.

Did cost cutting make the app worse?

I did not run a controlled quality comparison, so I cannot claim "no loss". What I can show: the reasoning setting was required by the model for tool use, so there was no alternative to test. Across my test runs, the number checker flagged 0 translation changes, and 9 of 9 safety checks passed. The one quality problem I found was a bug in my own search tool (it showed the cheapest product, not the one the buyer asked about, so the Matchmaker returned no results). I fixed it and re-tested three searches. The Bisaya translations are fluent in my reading but I have not had a native speaker check them yet.

Speed was also affected by design: showing a chat in Bisaya for the first time took 17 seconds, because each message was translated one after another. I changed this to translate them in parallel, and the same view now takes 1.3 seconds.

6. Safety

What could go wrong, and what I built

RiskDefense builtEvidence
Prompt injection / leaking data. A buyer tries to get a booth assistant to reveal the vendor's private bottom price.(1) The private price never enters the model's prompt: the assistant receives only public fields. (2) A pattern check flags obvious attacks and adds a warning to the prompt. (3) An output guard compares every reply to the private price and notes, and replaces any reply that contains them. (4) Messages are wrapped as data, not instructions. (5) The model provider's own content filter.Test: 3 attack messages, 0 leaks. Guard test: a reply containing the private price is caught. In the app, one injection was blocked by the provider's filter (shown in the safety log, Figure 6).
Invented facts / wrong action. The assistant makes up a price or term.The assistant must use quote_price for any price. If something is not in the vendor's data, it must say the vendor's team will follow up. Discounts are never offered.In the traced run, the price came from the tool ($1.20, $3,600). Attack tests got "a member of our sales team will follow up" instead of a number.
Translation changes a price or quantity.The Translator compares every number before and after, retries once, and shows a warning on the message if it still differs.Test: "5,000 bottles at $1.05 each, total $5,250" kept all numbers in Bisaya. The checker flags 1.05 vs 1.50.
Looping forever.Every agent has a hard limit of 4 tool rounds, then stops and logs it.Test with a stand-in model that asks for a tool forever: stopped after 5 calls and logged.
Overspending.80,000 tokens per session and 1.5 million per day, a limit of 15 AI requests per minute per session, and a 1,000-character message limit.Test: a session at its cap was refused ("This session has used its token allowance").

Limits I know about. Passwords reset by email only if SMTP email is configured; otherwise the app prints the reset link in the server terminal (I did not send a real email in testing). The lockout lets someone deliberately lock another person out for 5 minutes. Uploaded licenses are checked as PDFs but not scanned for malware. The booth assistant's pattern check only catches known phrasings, which is why the private price is kept out of the prompt entirely.

8. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • All vendors are fake demo data. There are no real products, orders, payments or contracts.
  • Zoom and Google Meet links are placeholders. Real links need a connected account.
  • The demo booth assistants accept every meeting and sample request automatically. Vendors can turn that off, but the approval screens have had less testing than the automatic path.
  • Translation quality has not been checked by native speakers, especially Bisaya, and product names are not translated.
  • Sign-in is basic: no email verification at sign-up, and reset emails need SMTP set up.
  • Only one person's untimed feedback so far (see Section 7), no pilot or measured task times.
  • The app stores data in a local SQLite file, which is fine for a prototype but not for many users.

If I had another month

  1. Run a timed pilot: my dad and two small manufacturers, measure time to find a supplier, and fix what confuses them. First, make the translation, ratings and Verified badge impossible to miss (my dad asked for features that already exist).
  2. Add what my dad asked for: shipping modes (air, sea, FCL, LCL), currency conversion, more product categories, similar-item updates by email, and WhatsApp or Messenger notifications.
  3. Have native speakers review Bisaya, Spanish, Vietnamese and Chinese translations of the common phrases.
  4. Real vendor onboarding: upload a price sheet or catalog and let the AI turn it into a booth, with the vendor approving every line.
  5. Real video-call links and calendar invites through Zoom and Google accounts.
  6. Email verification, a real database, and hosting that stays up.
  7. Photos, certificates and sample videos on each booth, plus order and quote tracking.
